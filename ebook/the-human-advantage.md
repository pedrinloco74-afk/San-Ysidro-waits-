# Introduction: The Human Question

Before breakfast, your phone has already made a dozen quiet decisions. It sorted messages, suggested a route, filtered a photograph, and chose which headlines to place at the top of your screen. At work, a writing assistant offers to tidy an email. At home, a streaming service has another episode ready before you have decided whether you want one.

None of these moments is dramatic on its own. Together, they raise a larger question: **Are your tools helping you choose, or choosing for you?**

Technology is often discussed as if it were weather: a force arriving from somewhere else, impossible to influence, something we can only endure or admire. But a tool is not the same thing as a destiny. People decide what to build, what to buy, what to automate, what to share, and what to refuse. Those choices can be small and ordinary. They still add up.

This book is for people who want to use modern technology—including artificial intelligence—without needing to become engineers, surrendering their judgment, or pretending every new product is either salvation or disaster. You will find practical ways to ask better questions, protect what matters, learn new tools, and keep human responsibility in the loop.

The central idea is simple: **start with the human purpose, then choose the technology that genuinely serves it.** Sometimes that will be an AI tool. Sometimes it will be a spreadsheet, a conversation, a paper list, or no new tool at all.

## A five-question pause

Throughout the book, we will return to a short check called **PAUSE**. Before you adopt a tool, share information, or trust an automated result, ask:

- **P — Purpose:** What am I trying to accomplish?
- **A — Access:** What information, permissions, or power does this tool need?
- **U — Uncertainty:** Where might it be wrong, incomplete, or misleading?
- **S — Stakes:** Who could be affected, and what would an error cost?
- **E — Exit:** Can I review, correct, appeal, undo, or leave?

You do not need a committee meeting to use PAUSE. A few seconds can be enough for a low-risk choice. Higher stakes deserve a slower, more careful review.

The chapters move from everyday choices to bigger ones: understanding the job a tool should do, working with AI, protecting attention and information, learning and working well, choosing services responsibly, and shaping the future as a citizen and community member. At the end, you will find a 30-day plan and a set of practical checklists.

You do not need to predict the future to meet it well. You need curiosity, boundaries, and the willingness to ask who a tool is for.

# Chapter 1: Begin With the Job, Not the Gadget

A new tool can be exciting. It can also create the illusion that you have solved a problem merely because you installed something. The first discipline of useful technology is therefore not learning the tool. It is naming the job.

Imagine that your team says, “We need AI.” That sounds like a plan, but it is really a label. What is difficult today? Are people waiting too long for answers? Repeating the same data entry? Searching through a mountain of notes? Trying to make a decision without enough context? Each problem calls for a different response. Some may be improved by software. Others may require clearer instructions, more time, better staffing, or a conversation that has been postponed.

A tool is a means. The outcome is the point.

## Notice the friction

For one week, keep a small “friction log.” Each time a task feels unnecessarily slow, confusing, repetitive, or stressful, write down four things:

1. What were you trying to do?
2. What made it harder than it needed to be?
3. How often does this happen, and who else is affected?
4. What would a genuinely better result look like?

The last question matters. “Use a clever app” is not a result. “Find the current schedule in under a minute” is. “Use a chatbot” is not a result. “Give a customer a correct answer without making them repeat their story” is.

A friction log makes invisible work visible. It can reveal that the supposed technology problem is actually a handoff problem, an unclear policy, or a process with three unnecessary approvals. Fixing the process first can make a later tool simpler, cheaper, and easier to trust.

## Separate the task from the decision

Technology is often good at moving, sorting, and transforming information. People remain essential where goals conflict, context is missing, values matter, or someone must answer for the result. These are not rigid boundaries; they are a useful starting point.

For any proposed automation, draw a line down a page. On the left, list the repeatable steps: collect a form, sort items by date, produce a first draft, flag a missing field. On the right, list the judgments: decide what is fair, interpret a person's circumstances, make a promise, approve a high-impact outcome, or explain why a choice was made.

Ask whether a tool can reduce the routine work while leaving the important judgment visible. If a system makes a decision difficult to inspect, the time saved may come with a hidden cost.

## Count the work around the work

A demo usually shows the moment when a tool succeeds. Everyday use also includes setup, training, checking, correcting, privacy review, maintenance, and the occasional recovery when something breaks. Those are part of the job too.

When comparing a new process with the old one, count the whole journey:

- How long does setup take?
- Who has to learn it, and who supports them?
- How will mistakes be spotted and fixed?
- What happens when the internet, account, or tool is unavailable?
- Can you get your information back if you leave?

A small tool that people understand can outperform a powerful system no one has time to maintain. “Easy to start” and “easy to live with” are different promises.

## Try this: run a tiny experiment

Choose one low-risk task from your friction log. Write down what happens now and what “better” means. Test one change with a small group or on a copy of the work. Set a short time limit. Compare the result with your starting point, including corrections and extra steps. Keep it, change it, or stop.

A good experiment is allowed to fail. The point is to learn before a small choice becomes an expensive commitment.

**Chapter takeaway:** Do not ask only, “What can this technology do?” Ask, “What human problem will be better if we use it?”

# Chapter 2: Work With AI, Not Under It

Artificial intelligence is a broad label for many different techniques. The tools most people meet first can generate or transform text, images, audio, and code from a request. Their fluency can feel like understanding. Fluency is useful—but it is not proof that an answer is true, complete, or appropriate.

A helpful mental model is to treat a generative AI assistant like a fast, tireless drafting partner whose work you still own. It can offer a starting point, a list of possibilities, or a clearer explanation. It can also miss a key detail, invent a source, repeat a bias, or sound certain while being wrong. The right level of review depends on the task.

## Give it a real brief

A vague request produces vague help. A good brief tells the tool what you need, what context matters, what constraints to respect, and what a useful result looks like. You can use this simple pattern:

- **Goal:** What should the output help you do?
- **Context:** What background is relevant? Leave out private details that are not needed.
- **Input:** What material should it work from?
- **Constraints:** What must it include, avoid, or preserve?
- **Review:** What should you check before using the result?

For example: “Turn the notes below into a short update for a neighborhood meeting. Keep the dates and names exactly as written. Use plain language, flag anything unclear instead of guessing, and finish with three questions I should verify.” This brief makes the job visible and tells the tool where not to improvise.

You can ask the assistant to show its assumptions, identify uncertainty, suggest alternatives, or critique a draft. That can make its limitations easier to see. It does not make the output self-verifying. A request to “double-check” is another request for text, not independent evidence.

## Keep the information boundary

Before pasting material into a service, ask what it contains and whether that service is approved for it. A name, account number, private message, unpublished plan, medical detail, or confidential work document may be sensitive even when it appears in an ordinary paragraph. Replace identifying details with placeholders when you can. If you cannot tell how submitted information is handled, do not assume it is private.

At work or school, follow the rules of the organization. A useful assistant is not worth exposing somebody else's information or breaking a promise you made to protect it.

## Verify in proportion to the stakes

For a low-risk task—brainstorming a birthday theme, for example—you may only need to choose what you like. For a fact that will be published, a number in a budget, a quotation, a legal requirement, a medical question, or a decision affecting someone's opportunity, check the claim against reliable sources and a qualified person where appropriate.

A practical review has four moves:

1. **Trace:** Can you find the original source, not just another page repeating the claim?
2. **Compare:** Does a second trustworthy source agree, especially on important details?
3. **Inspect:** Are names, dates, calculations, quotations, and links correct?
4. **Own:** Are you willing to stand behind the final result and explain how it was checked?

If a tool provides citations, open them. A citation-shaped link is not proof that the linked source says what the answer claims.

## Keep the skill you are borrowing

When you use AI to explain a subject, ask follow-up questions in your own words. When you use it to draft, edit the draft yourself. When you use it to solve a problem, try to explain the steps without looking. These small habits help you use a tool without letting a skill disappear from your hands.

A useful pattern is **attempt, assist, audit**: first try the part you want to learn; then invite the tool to help; finally compare its result with your reasoning and correct it. For routine work you already understand, you may move faster. For work you are learning, keep more of the thinking visible.

## Try this: a low-risk first use

Pick a task where an imperfect draft would be harmless. Ask an AI tool for three options, not one final answer. Compare them with your own goal. Note one helpful contribution and one thing you had to fix. If the tool did not save time after review, that is useful evidence too.

**Chapter takeaway:** AI can extend your reach, but responsibility does not transfer with the text. The person who uses the result remains responsible for how it is used.

# Chapter 3: Protect Your Attention

A device can be both a useful instrument and a beautifully designed interruption machine. The difference often comes down to who sets the defaults.

Many digital products compete for repeated attention. That does not mean every service is harmful, or that a person who gets distracted lacks discipline. It means the environment matters. A small number of deliberate settings can do more than a promise to “be better” tomorrow.

## Make the important thing easier to reach

Look at the first screen you see when you unlock your phone or open your browser. Does it show what you came to do, or what someone hopes you will click? Move the tools you value to a visible place. Put the most distracting ones a step farther away. Turn off alerts that do not need to interrupt you. Keep alerts for people or situations that truly require them.

This is not a moral test. It is arranging a room. You are more likely to use what is within reach.

Try a one-screen experiment for seven days: keep only essential alerts on, choose set times for news or social feeds, and leave one short stretch of the day device-free. At the end, ask what became easier and what you genuinely missed. Adjust rather than aiming for a perfect rule.

## Give your feeds a job

A feed can entertain, inform, connect, or sell. Sometimes it does several at once. Before opening one, choose a purpose: “I am checking messages from friends,” or “I am looking for the announcement.” When that purpose is met, close it.

For information you depend on, build a small source list you chose in advance: local reporting, a subject expert, an official notice, a community organization, or a publication with clear corrections. A mix of sources makes it easier to notice when one stream leaves something out. Slow down when a post creates a strong urge to share it immediately; urgency can be part of the message's design.

Ask three quick questions of an alarming or astonishing claim: Who first reported this? What evidence is offered? What would change my mind? If you cannot answer, wait before forwarding. Waiting is not indifference. It is a way to avoid making somebody else's unverified claim your own.

## Design for stopping

Useful tools make it clear when a task is complete. A feed that never ends can make stopping feel like a decision you have to make again and again. Create a stopping cue: set a timer, choose a final item, sign out after a task, or put the device on a charger outside the room. Pair a digital activity with a natural boundary, such as “check messages after lunch, then return to the report.”

If technology is interfering with sleep, relationships, school, work, or your sense of well-being, a settings change may help—but it is not the only kind of support. Talk with someone you trust or a qualified professional if you need help making a larger change.

## Try this: a seven-day attention reset

Choose one app or service. Before opening it, write down why you are going in. Disable one nonessential alert. Set a time or stopping point. After seven days, keep only the changes that made your day better. The goal is not to use technology as little as possible; it is to spend attention on purpose.

**Chapter takeaway:** Your attention is part of your life. Set defaults that make it easier to give that attention where you want it.

# Chapter 4: Protect Information and Build Trust

Privacy is not simply a secret to hide. It is the ability to choose what information about you is shared, with whom, and for what purpose. Information about one person can also reveal things about a family, workplace, or community. That makes privacy a matter of respect as well as personal preference.

No single setting can protect everything. A few sturdy habits, practiced consistently, make a better foundation than chasing every alarming headline.

## Use a simple information test

Before sharing information with an app, website, or AI service, ask:

- Does this service need this information to do the job?
- Would I be comfortable if the information were seen by someone other than the intended reader?
- Can I use a less identifying version?
- Can I remove the information later, and do I know how?

Treat private information about other people with the same care you want for your own. Ask before posting a photo, forwarding a message, or entering someone else's story into a tool. Consent matters even when the technology makes sharing effortless.

## Make accounts harder to take over

For important accounts, use a different, hard-to-guess password for each one. A reputable password manager can help you create and store unique passwords without having to memorize them all. Turn on multi-factor authentication where it is available, especially for email, financial, work, and account-recovery services. If passkeys are offered and fit your needs, learn how they work before changing a system you rely on.

Keep your phone, computer, browser, and apps updated. Updates can include security fixes as well as new features. Back up important files somewhere separate from the device that holds the originals, and occasionally check that you can restore them. A backup you have never tested is only a hope.

Be cautious with unexpected links, attachments, urgent payment requests, and requests to reveal a code. If a message claims to come from a bank, employer, delivery service, or family member and something feels off, contact that person or organization through a channel you already trust—not the link or number in the suspicious message.

## Match protection to risk

Not every account needs the same amount of effort. Start with the accounts that can unlock other accounts: your primary email and phone account are often important because they are used for password resets. Next, protect money, work, and personal records. Then review what information is public and remove anything you no longer want exposed, where the service allows it.

If you are targeted, handle sensitive work, or face a specific safety risk, generic tips may not be enough. Get advice tailored to your situation from a qualified security professional or trusted support organization. The right plan depends on who might be trying to access your information and what they could do with it.

## Trust requires an exit

A service earns trust not merely by saying “secure,” but by making its practices understandable and giving users meaningful choices. Can you see what you have shared? Can you download your data? Can you delete an account? Is it clear how to report a mistake or get help? Can a human review a consequential decision?

These questions matter for individuals and for organizations choosing tools on other people's behalf. Convenience is valuable. So is the ability to leave without losing everything you created.

## Try this: secure one important account

Choose your primary email account. Check that its password is unique. Turn on an additional sign-in protection method if available. Review recovery options and make sure they still belong to you. Then check whether you can access a recent backup of one important file.

**Chapter takeaway:** Trust grows when people know what they are sharing, how they are protected, and what they can do if something goes wrong.

# Chapter 5: Learn, Work, and Keep Your Craft

When a new technology arrives, people often ask, “Will it take my job?” It is a reasonable question, but work is made of many tasks, relationships, and responsibilities. A tool may change one part of a job, create new work around another, and leave the most important human judgment untouched. The outcome depends on the task, the workplace, the people affected, and the choices made during adoption.

Rather than trying to predict one dramatic future, look closely at the work in front of you.

## Treat automation as a change to a process

For any task a tool might handle, ask three questions:

1. **What part is routine?** Is there a repeatable step that can be safely sped up?
2. **What part is judgment?** Where do context, relationships, ethics, or accountability matter?
3. **What support will people need?** Who reviews the result, fixes errors, and learns the new workflow?

A tool that saves ten minutes while creating a hidden hour of checking has not necessarily improved the job. A tool that makes a difficult service accessible to more people might be valuable even if it does not make a task faster. Choose measures that reflect the outcome people actually care about.

## Use the “draft, decide, explain” pattern

For work where AI assistance is permitted, let the tool produce a draft or options. A person then decides what to keep, correct, or reject. Finally, that person should be able to explain the reasoning behind the result. This keeps authorship and accountability clear.

The pattern is especially important when a result affects a person’s money, opportunity, reputation, health, education, or access to a service. A human name on a decision is not meaningful oversight if the person cannot inspect the information or change the outcome. Human review must come with time, authority, and a real chance to disagree.

## Keep expertise alive

A shortcut can help you do more. It can also make it harder to notice when the shortcut is wrong. If you use a tool to prepare a report, keep the source records. If you use it to write code, test the code. If you use it to teach a concept, ask learners to apply the concept themselves. The goal is not to forbid assistance. It is to preserve enough understanding to supervise it.

For a new skill, try this learning loop:

- Make an honest attempt before asking for the answer.
- Ask the tool to explain, demonstrate, or quiz you rather than simply finish for you.
- Check the result against a reliable reference or someone with expertise.
- Repeat the work without the tool once in a while, so you can tell what you still know.

This can make assistance more powerful, because you become better at asking, evaluating, and adapting rather than only copying.

## Make change a conversation

In a workplace, the people closest to a process often know where it breaks. Invite them into testing before a purchase is finalized. Explain what will be measured, what will not be measured, and how workers can report harm or confusion. Do not frame a tool as a neutral mandate if it changes how people are evaluated or how their information is used.

A successful implementation is not only a technical launch. It is a working agreement: what the tool may do, who checks it, how errors are handled, what training is offered, and who has the authority to pause it.

## Try this: map one task

Pick a recurring task at work, school, or home. Mark which steps are routine, which require judgment, and who bears the cost of an error. If you test a tool, have a person compare the result with the old process and keep a record of corrections. Ask the people who do the task whether it improved their day—not only whether it made a dashboard look faster.

**Chapter takeaway:** The best use of automation does not erase human expertise; it gives people more room to apply it where it matters.

# Chapter 6: Keep Relationships and Communities in View

A message can travel farther than intended. A recommendation can shape what a group sees. A camera can record a moment that one person wants to remember and another person would rather keep private. Technology connects people, but connection is not the same as care.

A healthy digital life includes the people who are not holding the device.

## Ask before you capture and share

Before you photograph, record, quote, or post, pause long enough to ask who is present and what the consequences might be. Children, patients, students, employees, and people in vulnerable situations may not feel free to say no. A quick “Is it okay if I share this?” is a start, but the power difference matters too. Sometimes the respectful choice is not to publish.

Set family or group expectations that are simple enough to remember: which pictures stay private, where location details are shared, what goes into a group chat, and how someone can ask for a post to be removed. Review those expectations as children grow and circumstances change.

## Make room for different needs

People do not all use a screen, read a message, or navigate a service in the same way. Someone may need captions, large text, a keyboard instead of a touchscreen, clear language, a slower process, or the option to speak with a person. Accessibility helps people participate; it is not an optional decoration added after the important decisions.

When choosing a service for a group, invite people with different needs to test it. Ask what happens if a person does not own a recent phone, has unreliable internet, shares a device, or cannot use the default interface. Keep another route available for essential tasks. A digital-only path can turn a convenience for some into a barrier for others.

## Use devices without making them the whole relationship

A video call can let a grandparent join a family gathering from far away. It does not have to replace the visit, the call, or the quiet moment afterward. A classroom tool can help someone practice. It should not crowd out a teacher noticing that a student is confused. A group chat can organize neighbors. It cannot always replace the trust built by showing up.

The point is not to divide the world into “real” and “digital.” Digital relationships are real relationships. The point is to choose a medium that fits the human need. Some moments call for speed; some call for privacy; some call for a voice, a face, or shared physical space.

## Try this: write a household technology agreement

In one short conversation, ask each person: What do our devices help us do? What gets in the way? What information should stay private? What is one time or place we want to protect for being together? Write down a few agreements and revisit them in a month. Make them mutual: adults model the boundaries they ask children to follow.

**Chapter takeaway:** Good technology choices consider the people around the user—not just the person clicking the button.

# Chapter 7: Choose Tools That Deserve a Place in Your Life

A polished demonstration shows what a product can do under favorable conditions. A good decision asks what it does on an ordinary day, with your actual information, your real constraints, and the people who will live with the result.

You can use PAUSE to evaluate almost any service, from a note-taking app to a system used to allocate public resources.

## Walk through PAUSE

**Purpose:** What specific need does the tool meet? How will you know whether it helped?

**Access:** What data does it collect? What permissions does it ask for? Can you limit access to what the task requires?

**Uncertainty:** What kinds of mistakes does it make? Can you see the basis for important outputs? Is the result tested with people like the people who will rely on it?

**Stakes:** Who benefits if it works? Who bears the cost if it fails? Could the tool make a choice harder to challenge, or treat some people unfairly?

**Exit:** Can you correct or appeal an outcome? Can you export your information and move to another tool? Is there a clear way to stop using it?

If the answers are unclear, that is a reason to ask more questions—not necessarily a reason to panic. For a low-stakes trial, uncertainty may be acceptable. For a consequential decision, unanswered questions are a stop sign.

## Look beyond the sticker price

The cost of a tool includes more than its monthly fee. Count the time spent learning it, the equipment it requires, the support it needs, the information it gathers, and the consequences of leaving. A service may appear inexpensive because another person is doing unpaid setup or cleanup. A free app may be funded in a way that makes your attention or data part of the product. Read the terms that matter, and do not be afraid to ask a provider a direct question.

For an organization, involve the people who will use the tool and the people whose information it touches. Define a small pilot with a clear end date. Decide in advance what would count as success, what would trigger a pause, who reviews complaints, and how the organization will communicate a failure. Do not allow “the algorithm did it” to become an excuse without an accountable person behind it.

## Keep a way back

Before relying on a new service, know what happens if it is unavailable. Keep a copy of important work in a format you can use elsewhere. Save the original record when a generated or automated result matters. Maintain an offline option for essential information if that is practical. A fallback is not an insult to technology; it is resilience.

## Try this: the five-minute buying pause

Before your next technology purchase or sign-up, answer the five PAUSE questions in writing. If you cannot explain the purpose in one sentence, wait a day. If you can, compare one alternative—including doing nothing differently—and choose the option whose benefits and costs you can actually describe.

**Chapter takeaway:** A worthy tool gives you useful capability without hiding its limits, collecting more than it needs, or trapping you when your needs change.

# Chapter 8: The Future Is a Set of Choices

No person can control every technology decision. A user may not choose the design of a platform, the rules at work, or the systems a government adopts. But limited control is not no control. People can ask questions, support better practices, set boundaries, and make their experience visible to the people who build and govern tools.

## Be a participant, not only a consumer

When a product fails, describe the task you were trying to do, the step that failed, and the effect it had. Clear feedback is more useful than a general complaint. When a workplace or school proposes a new system, ask how it will affect people who are often overlooked. When a local service moves online, ask what route remains for people who cannot use it. When a product works well, say which feature made a difference.

If you have influence over a purchase or policy, use it to protect basics: understandable notices, appropriate data limits, accessibility, human review for important decisions, and a meaningful way to appeal. These are not anti-technology demands. They are the conditions that make technology trustworthy.

## Build a personal technology compass

Write down three things technology should help you do, such as learn, create, stay in touch, or serve your community. Then write three things you do not want it to take from you, such as privacy, rest, independent judgment, or access to a human being. Keep the list where you make choices.

The list will change as your life changes. That is healthy. A tool that helped during one season may stop fitting in the next. You are allowed to revise your relationship with it.

## Practice informed optimism

Optimism does not mean believing every promise. Skepticism does not mean rejecting every possibility. Informed optimism says: let us try what may help, in a way that lets us see what happens, limits preventable harm, and keeps the option to change course.

That is a practical way to meet a changing world. You do not have to become an expert in every new system. You can learn enough to ask good questions, seek evidence, and choose with intention. You can also ask other people to help. Shared judgment is often stronger than isolated confidence.

**Chapter takeaway:** The future of technology is shaped not only by what can be built, but by what people choose to accept, improve, and refuse.

# Conclusion: Keep the Human in the Loop

A useful tool can make a hard task lighter, help a quiet voice be heard, or give you a new way to create. It can also take attention, expose information, hide an error, or make a decision harder to question. The same tool can do both, depending on how it is designed and used.

That is why the best question is rarely, “Is this technology good or bad?” A better question is, “Good for whom, for what purpose, under which conditions, and with what way to put things right?”

You have seen how to begin with the job, work with AI carefully, protect attention and information, preserve skill, include the people affected, and evaluate a tool before it becomes a dependency. None of these practices requires perfect knowledge. They require attention to purpose and a willingness to review the result.

When a new system appears, take a PAUSE. Name the purpose. Limit access. Look for uncertainty. Match the level of care to the stakes. Keep an exit open. Then decide.

Technology should enlarge human possibility—not quietly narrow it. The human advantage is not that people never make mistakes. It is that people can notice, learn, care about consequences, and choose again.

# Appendix A: The PAUSE Card

Keep this page nearby when you try a new tool, share sensitive information, or rely on an automated result.

## P — Purpose

- What am I trying to accomplish?
- What would a genuinely useful result look like?
- Is a new technology the simplest way to get there?

## A — Access

- What information or permissions does the tool need?
- Can I provide less?
- Do I have permission to share information about other people?

## U — Uncertainty

- Where could the tool be wrong or incomplete?
- Can I inspect the source or basis for its result?
- What should I verify independently?

## S — Stakes

- Who could be affected?
- What is the cost of an error?
- Is a human with time and authority reviewing important decisions?

## E — Exit

- Can I correct, appeal, undo, export, or delete?
- What is the fallback if the service fails?
- Can I leave without losing work or access I need?

**Rule of thumb:** The higher the stakes, the more evidence, review, and human accountability you need.

# Appendix B: A 30-Day Technology Reset

This plan is a menu, not a test. Skip steps that do not fit your life. Ten minutes is enough for many of them.

## Week 1: Notice

**Day 1:** Write down three things you want technology to help you do.

**Day 2:** Keep a friction log for one day. Note one task that feels harder than necessary.

**Day 3:** Look at your phone's home screen. Move one distracting app and one useful app to places that match your intentions.

**Day 4:** Turn off one alert that rarely deserves to interrupt you.

**Day 5:** Choose a time of day when you would like fewer screens. Try a small device-free window.

**Day 6:** Review one feed. Add a source you trust or remove one that consistently leaves you tense without informing you.

**Day 7:** Reflect: What change helped? What felt unrealistic? Keep only what is useful.

## Week 2: Protect

**Day 8:** Identify your most important email account and check its sign-in protections.

**Day 9:** Replace one reused or weak password with a unique one. Consider a password manager if it suits you.

**Day 10:** Review account recovery methods. Remove outdated phone numbers or addresses.

**Day 11:** Check that automatic updates are on for a device you rely on.

**Day 12:** Back up one important file to a separate location.

**Day 13:** Review which apps can access your location, contacts, camera, or microphone. Remove access that no longer makes sense.

**Day 14:** Ask whether you could restore one backed-up file. Make a note of what still needs a backup plan.

## Week 3: Use tools deliberately

**Day 15:** Pick a low-risk task where an AI assistant could help. Write down what “help” means first.

**Day 16:** Try a specific prompt with a clear goal and constraints.

**Day 17:** Check the result. Record one thing it got right and one thing you needed to fix.

**Day 18:** Try asking the tool to explain its assumptions or list what needs verification.

**Day 19:** Practice a task yourself before asking the tool to do it. Compare the approaches.

**Day 20:** Review your school or workplace rules for AI and sensitive information.

**Day 21:** Decide whether the tool earned a place in this task. You do not have to keep using it.

# Appendix C: Prompts That Keep You in Charge

These examples are starting points. Use them only with information you are allowed to share, and check the result before relying on it.

## To organize notes

“Organize the notes below into a short outline. Keep names, dates, and figures exactly as written. Mark missing or contradictory details as questions; do not guess.”

## To brainstorm

“Give me five different approaches to [goal]. For each, list one advantage, one drawback, and one assumption. Do not choose for me.”

## To make something clearer

“Rewrite this paragraph for a general audience. Preserve the meaning and any qualifications. After the rewrite, list anything you changed that might affect the meaning.”

## To learn a concept

“Explain [concept] with a simple example, then ask me three questions to check my understanding. Wait for my answers before showing solutions.”

## To review a draft

“Review this draft for clarity and missing steps. Do not add facts. Separate your suggestions from the original text, and identify anything I should verify with a reliable source.”

## To prepare for a decision

“Help me compare these options using the criteria I provide. Point out trade-offs and missing information. Do not make the final choice.”

## To build a checklist

“Turn this process description into a checklist. Keep every required step. Mark uncertain steps for human confirmation rather than inventing a procedure.”

# Appendix D: A Small Glossary

**Accessibility:** Designing a product, service, or place so people with different abilities can use it and participate.

**Algorithm:** A set of steps or rules a computer follows to process information or perform a task.

**Artificial intelligence (AI):** A broad term for computer systems designed to perform tasks associated with human abilities such as recognizing patterns, generating content, or making predictions. Different AI systems work in different ways.

**Automation:** Using a system to carry out some or all steps of a task with less direct human effort.

**Data minimization:** Collecting or sharing only the information needed for a specific purpose.

**Generative AI:** AI systems that produce new content—such as text, images, audio, or code—in response to a request or other input.

**Interoperability:** The ability of different systems to exchange information and work together.

**Multi-factor authentication:** A sign-in method that asks for more than one kind of proof that you are the account owner.

**Passkey:** A sign-in credential designed to replace a password on supported services and devices. The exact setup and recovery options vary by service.
