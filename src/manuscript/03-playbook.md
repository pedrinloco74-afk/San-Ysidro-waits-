# PART THREE
## The Playbook

# Chapter 7: The Phone

The phone is where the answer is. It is also where people commit crimes, get thrown out of their homes, and lose custody battles. So we do this properly.

Everything below is either (a) something that is legally open to you as a **named account holder**, or (b) something you observe **without breaking in**. Read Chapter 5 again if you're tempted to skip that distinction.

## Route 1 — The Records Everyone Forgets

Carriers, platforms, and banks keep logs. You may be entitled to see them.

:::check THE PAPER-FIRST PATH
- [ ] **Open the account.** If you are a named user on the mobile account, log in and look at the **itemized call and text detail**, if your carrier provides it. Look for one number that repeats at odd hours, or a pattern of texts at 6:40 a.m.
- [ ] **Check the family plan.** Numbers you don't recognize. Lines you didn't know were added.
- [ ] **Download the bills as PDFs** and save the last six months. These are your own bills; keep them.
- [ ] **Look at the timing, not just the names.** The most revealing thing in a call log is 90 minutes at 11 p.m. — not the contact name.
- [ ] **Check the device count.** Most phone accounts list every device and its purchase date. A second device is a very loud sentence.
- [ ] **Look at the data usage pattern**, if the account shows it. Sudden spikes, sudden drops, mystery hotspots.
:::

## Route 2 — Shared Accounts You Are Already In

Family iCloud, shared Google, shared photo albums, a shared laptop, the "family" calendar, the shared streaming account, the shared Amazon account. You may already be inside these legitimately.

:::donow WHAT TO LOOK FOR IN A SHARED ACCOUNT
- **Photo locations.** Moments, "Places," recent uploads, deleted items folder (deleted photos often sit there for 30 days).
- **Calendar invitations** that don't appear in your partner's story. Look for recurring blocks, "busy" events with no title, and location hints in the invitation.
- **Shared notes and reminders.** Shopping lists are boring — but look for addresses, dates, and names.
- **Maps history**, if you're the account owner: recent searches, saved places, "your timeline."
- **Purchase history** in a shared store account: restaurant orders, rides, hotel nights, gifts, flowers, subscriptions.
:::

:::legal KEEP YOUR HANDS OFF IF YOU'RE NOT SURE
"Shared" is doing a lot of work in that list, and the law draws lines that your feelings don't care about.

The safe version: what's **in your own account**, on **your own device**, in **your own name**, that you're an **authorized user** of.

The dangerous version: an account that is *theirs*, a device that is *theirs*, a password that *they* set up, a locked phone you got in by guessing a four-digit code. In some states a spouse can be charged for exactly that. Ask a lawyer before you log in anywhere that has your partner's name alone on the top of the page.
:::

## Route 3 — Observation, Not Invasion

This is legal almost everywhere, and it is where the real catches happen.

- **The kitchen counter test.** Devices left out, screens visible while you're in the room, notifications appearing on a locked screen. Noticing a notification that shows on a screen you were already looking at is observation. Picking up a locked phone and guessing a passcode is not.
- **The car.** What's in the passenger door pocket, the center console, the trunk, the glove box. **Not** what a tracker records, unless your state allows tracking on a vehicle you own — and even then, get advice first.
- **The laundry.** Sheets, shirts, receipts in pockets, hotel key cards, an unfamiliar scent or perfume, a specific brand of lipstick on a collar, sand in the carpet in November.
- **The doorbell and home cameras.** Your own footage, of your own driveway and door. Note the times they claim to have left or arrived, and compare.
- **The charge cable.** A charger in the car that isn't for a phone you know about. An extra wall plug in a nightstand drawer.

:::donow THE TWO-MINUTE CHECKLIST FOR ANY DEVICE YOU OWN
- [ ] Notification previews — what appeared while you were in the room? Write it down immediately.
- [ ] Case, wallet, glovebox, bag pockets. Anything new, anything odd.
- [ ] Shared family tracking app that *both* of you joined — check location history. If you're both on it, using it is fair.
- [ ] Bluetooth lists in a car you own. A paired device name that isn't anyone in the family is a lead, not proof.
- [ ] Recent rides and delivery orders in a shared account.
:::

## The Three Things That Give It Away on Any Phone

Even from across the room, a phone tells you its secrets in the way it's used. Watch the **hands**, not the screen.

1. **Angling.** A phone that gets tilted away from you, even slightly, when a message arrives.
2. **Timing.** A phone that lights up and is picked up within a second and a half. Nobody picks up a spam email that fast.
3. **Deletion gymnastics.** A person who clears a chat right after reading it, or has "no notifications" for exactly one conversation.

Those three habits show up in nearly every case. They are not proof — they're *behavioral tells*, and they tell you exactly where to look next, **legally**.

:::watch THE ONE MISTAKE THAT ENDS THE CASE
Don't confront based on a phone you looked at illegally. In most cases you've now given them the perfect counter-story: *"They broke into my phone. I don't know what they saw. Ask them why they violated my privacy."*

That story works on family, it works on friends, and it works in front of a judge.
:::

# Chapter 8: Follow the Money

Money is the most honest witness in this book. A cheater can control their face, their story, their phone, and their friends. They cannot stop the bank from writing things down.

If you share finances, you have legitimate access to shared accounts — use it. Start with six months and work forward.

:::check THE MONEY TRAIL CHECKLIST
- [ ] **Card and account statements.** Look for repeat charges. The same restaurant, the same gas station, the same neighborhood — different nights.
- [ ] **Restaurant charges for two.** Especially on nights you were told they were working, at the gym, or with "the guys."
- [ ] **Hotel, motel, and short-term rental charges.** Even small ones. Parking garages in a city you weren't in. A valet charge is a confession with a timestamp.
- [ ] **ATM withdrawals in odd amounts.** Cash is what people use when they don't want a receipt — and repeated $60 and $80 withdrawals are a pattern, not a habit.
- [ ] **Gift and flower purchases** with no gift arriving at your house.
- [ ] **Subscription charges.** Dating apps, additional phone lines, cloud storage for a second device, "private" photo apps, a second streaming account.
- [ ] **Fitness, salon, spa, and grooming expenses** that appear after the schedule changed.
- [ ] **Travel.** Flights, rental cars, gas stations far from home, tolls with times attached, hotel loyalty points.
- [ ] **Grocery and alcohol patterns.** Two bottles of wine at 9 p.m. on a Tuesday when you weren't there. Dinner for two at a grocery deli.
- [ ] **Cash-app style transfers.** Many of these services make public or semi-public feeds. Look for repeated sends, cute memo lines, and the same name.
- [ ] **New accounts, new cards, and loans.** Look at your credit report — if you're on joint accounts, this is your business as much as theirs. If you're not, a lawyer or your own credit file can still tell you a great deal.
:::

:::donow HOW TO READ A STATEMENT LIKE A DETECTIVE
Print or export six months. Then:

1. **Highlight every charge that you can't personally account for.** Not "unusual" — unaccounted for.
2. **Group them by day of week.** Cheaters have schedules. Monday and Thursday will start to glow.
3. **Group them by location.** One neighborhood that keeps appearing.
4. **Check the times.** A charge at 10:40 p.m. forty minutes from home is a fact. A charge at 6:15 a.m. on a Sunday is a fact.
5. **Look for the boring stuff.** Two coffees at a gas station at 11 p.m. is your most useful receipt — it puts two people in one car at one time.
6. **Match each one against what you were told that day.** Write down the story next to the charge. The gap between the two is your case.
:::

:::legal THE FINANCIAL BOUNDARY
- **Shared accounts:** fair game — you're a named account holder.
- **Accounts in their name only:** do not log in, do not impersonate them on a call, do not open their mail if that's illegal where you live. Ask a lawyer about discovery in a divorce — a subpoena can get what a hacking charge cannot.
- **Their employer's accounts:** never. That's a different set of crimes.
- **The family business:** depends on your role. Get advice.
:::

:::truth THE ONE NUMBER THAT MATTERS MOST
Find the repeating number. Not the one big charge — the small one that shows up every week, same plaza, same time window.

Repeat beats size. A $9.40 charge, thirteen times, is a *pattern*. A single $400 charge is a *story*.
:::

# Chapter 9: The Time and Place Audit

Affairs are not just emotional events; they're logistics. Two people have to be in the same place at the same time without witnesses, and that requires time that has to be stolen from somewhere.

You can find the stolen time.

## Step 1 — Build the Master Timeline

Take the last 90 days. For each day, write down:

:::table THE MASTER TIMELINE
**Claimed** | Where they said they were, for how long, with whom.
**Actual known** | Where you know they were — from your own memory, shared calendar, work schedule, or receipts.
**Gap** | The hours that don't fit either column.
**Money** | Any charge that lands in the gap.
**Story** | What they said afterwards, in their own words.
:::

You're looking for gaps that repeat. Once is scheduling. Three times is a habit.

## Step 2 — Do the Math on the Commute

This is the single most underrated tool in the book, and it requires no devices at all.

- **Odometer and mileage.** A car that gained 240 miles on a day they say they drove 40 is a fact you can write down. Note it. Photograph the odometer on a normal day and compare.
- **Fuel.** A full tank every three days, when they only drive twelve miles a day, means somebody is moving.
- **Tolls and parking apps.** Timestamps are included. Both directions.
- **Ride history.** In a shared account, each trip has a start, an end, and a time.
- **Their own words about a trip.** "It took forever" is a gift. It means there's a route, and routes have lengths.

## Step 3 — Audit "Work"

Not by calling the office. That's a shortcut to humiliation if you're wrong — and to a career problem if you're right.

- **The work calendar they share with you** versus the hours they claim to be at work.
- **The training, the conference, the offsite.** Do these appear in any company-wide email, any photo, any badge photo, any receipt?
- **The commute claim.** "I stayed late" — but came home smelling of nothing and with a fresh haircut at 11 p.m.
- **The work "friend."** The person who is always mentioned in passing, whose name appears in a story that doesn't need them in it.
- **Their work phone and work email.** Do not touch them. Ever. That's their employer's property and touching it is a career-and-court problem. You can, however, note what they tell you and what shows up on a public LinkedIn post about the same trip.

## Step 4 — Audit the Routine Breakers

The best tells are the changes to things that used to be sacred.

:::check THE ROUTINE-BREAKERS
- [ ] **Gym time that no longer matches the gym.** Especially if the gym is a 6-minute drive and they're gone for two hours.
- [ ] **Errands that take twice as long as the errand.** The store is twelve minutes round trip; they were gone for ninety.
- [ ] **"Going for a drive to clear my head,"** new in the last three months.
- [ ] **Their weekend sport** suddenly has a longer schedule, an away game, a new team.
- [ ] **Church, meeting, class, or club** attendance that only they can confirm.
- [ ] **The "I'm being dropped off" trick.** Someone else drives. Someone else's car is in your driveway at odd hours.
- [ ] **Their phone's location sharing is off,** but yours was never on in the first place.
- [ ] **Sick days and half-days.** Sudden dentist appointments. A "mental health day" that you can't verify and that ends with a shower at 4 p.m.
:::

:::donow THE 20-MINUTE JOURNAL EXERCISE
For the next two weeks, write one line per day: *where they said they were, and what I actually saw, heard, or can prove.*

Do not interpret a single entry. Do not argue with the entries. Just write them.

At the end of fourteen days, read them all at once, in one sitting. That's the moment people describe as "the floor dropping out." Every time — for readers whose partner was cheating, and for readers who discover their partner was simply drowning at work and hiding it badly.
:::

:::watch THE TWO WAYS THIS GOES WRONG
**You start interrogating.** An interrogation teaches them to lie better. A calm question gets you information; a cross-examination gets you a performance.

**You count your own good behavior as evidence.** Them being home at 6 p.m. two nights in a row after you found something is not a fix. That's a *reset*, and resets usually last exactly as long as the fear does.
:::

# Chapter 10: The Internet Never Forgot

Here is the beautiful, terrible part. Most of the evidence you need is already public, and the person who posted it did the work for you.

Public information is legal everywhere. If they put it on the open internet — or a friend did, or a stranger's photo caught them in the background — that's a lead you can chase in your pajamas.

:::check THE PUBLIC TRAIL
- [ ] **Their public profiles and post history.** Not just the timeline. Check the *likes* and *comments* — the person whose posts they like within thirty seconds of publication is a person with notifications turned on.
- [ ] **Tagged photos and check-ins.** Especially anything from a "work trip," a "boys' weekend," or a "family thing."
- [ ] **Photos taken *by other people*.** This is where amateurs get caught. Friends post, you compare timestamps and places, and the story that your partner told collapses.
- [ ] **Photo backgrounds.** Reflections, mirrors, a second coffee cup, a napkin with a logo, a hotel room that isn't a hotel you know, the shape of a necklace that isn't yours.
- [ ] **Their follow lists and followers.** New women or men who appeared in the last three months. Look at who they followed and when, where visible.
- [ ] **Their friends' public content.** The "work group" photo. The dinner table. The person standing in the background.
- [ ] **Reviews and business profiles.** Google profiles show reviews, photo uploads, and sometimes check-ins. A "great stay" review signed by your partner with a date attached is a masterpiece of evidence.
- [ ] **Public dating profiles.** Check the major apps and sites for an active profile with their photo. Be aware: profiles can be old and left up, and images can be stolen. Screenshot with the date visible, but treat a single stale profile as a lead, not a conclusion.
- [ ] **Public records.** Property, business licenses, professional licenses, marriage records, court records, evictions, small claims, protective orders. All free, all legit, all searchable.
- [ ] **Professional footprints.** LinkedIn job history, conferences, speaking photos, tagged announcements — the best timeline of "work trips" you'll ever find. Conference agendas are published; so are the speaker lists.
- [ ] **Their stories versus their history.** The "I've never been to that city" claim that contradicts a public photo.
:::

:::donow REVERSE IMAGE SEARCH
Save a photo of the other person — public, from a public post — and run a reverse image search. It's how a name, a workplace, or a second profile appears when everything else is hidden. It's free, it takes two minutes, and it's completely legal.

Do not use it to contact anyone. Do not post it. Do not build a shrine. Write down what you found and move on.
:::

:::legal THE LINE YOU DO NOT CROSS ONLINE
- **Do not make a fake account to talk to them or to your partner.** Impersonation, catfishing, and "I was just testing them" schemes are dishonest, often illegal, and always self-destructive. Also, in half the states they can be used against you.
- **Do not log into their accounts** even if you know the password.
- **Do not post anything.** Not a vague quote, not a photo, not a "some people are so fake" rant. That's evidence against *you*, and it can even be defamation.
- **Do not contact the other person.** Not to confront, not to warn, not to "get the truth." You'll be lied to, or worse — you'll give the two of them something to bond over.
- **Do not message the other person's spouse or partner** without talking to a lawyer first. It can be the right thing. It can also detonate your case and your safety. Ask.
:::

:::truth THE 2 A.M. RULE
Anything you find after 2 a.m. feels 400% more conclusive than it is.

Photograph it, log it, and go to sleep. Tomorrow morning you'll see what it actually is — evidence, coincidence, or your own heart doing math.
:::
